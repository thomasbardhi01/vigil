# Review saturates first; Vigil's verifier is the lever

Research cut-off 2026-10-30. Extends `/home/claude/reports/Vigil factory research Oct 2026.md` (E1–E11, N1–N3) and `/home/claude/reports/Amp and Warp philosophy for Vigil.md` (V1–V8), and edits the build list at `/home/claude/reports/coord-prompt-post-phase-2.md`. Every claim carries the URL the notes recorded; where the notes found nothing, this says so.

**Bottom line.** The owner's hypothesis is half right. When generation is cheap the constraint moves to verification, but the measured part is *human review and quality*, not CI minutes: Faros's 22,000-developer telemetry shows task throughput +33.7% against median time in review **+441.5%**, bugs per developer **+54%**, incidents-to-PR **+242.7%**, and PRs merged with no review **+31.3%** ([Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-acceleration-whiplash-takeaways)); DORA says "the bottleneck shifts to reviewers" and that test automation may return more than optimising review ([dora.dev](https://dora.dev/insights/balancing-ai-tensions/)). No dataset ties CI wait or cost to AI adoption; that claim is argued, not measured. Proton's answer, CEO Andy Yen's line (not the CTO's) that engineers will "write specs for tests that AI codes and runs" with no verification process described ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/manifestos-fear-and-loser-thinking-inside-protons-ai-reckoning/)), is the inversion Vigil forbids, and the numbers say why: a single passing generated test has accept-precision **0.30** ([Groundability](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.01023)), **61.9%** of judge-endorsed generated tests failed on the gold patch, and **28.5%** of audited SWE-bench Verified tasks accept a wrong patch ([arXiv 2606.16062](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.16062)). The techniques with the best evidence and fit, in order: give the reviewer execution evidence (an 8B model reaches 0.98 catch / 0.00 over-rejection with it, 0.28–0.70 without, at every size), make the replay eval statistically real (a 20-task single run is ±10–15 points of noise), cut the verifier's own cost without cutting coverage (base-run cache, flake policy, name-based test selection safe on 99.6% of commits), harden the hackability audit, and use the local model first as a best-of-n candidate generator and grounded reviewer rather than a solo worker (a 32B model goes 42%→59% when a host verifier picks among 16 samples, [Together](https://www.together.ai/blog/deepswe)). Warp's benchmark is worth copying for its structure and cadence, not its LLM judge. The build list needs about a dozen changes, listed in section 6 with the source each rests on; the most consequential is that the $200/month envelope cannot power Phase C's three replay arms at once.

## 1. The bottleneck is review capacity and defect rate, not CI minutes

Three independent sources measure the same shape. Faros's 2026 report, from **22,000 developers and more than 4,000 teams** over two years, finds high-AI cohorts shipping +33.7% tasks and +16.2% merged PRs per developer while median time to first review rises **+156.6%**, average time in review **+199.6%** and median time in review **+441.5%**; churn +861%, bugs per developer +54% (from +9% in the 2025 edition), incident-to-PR ratio +242.7% ([Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-acceleration-whiplash-takeaways)). Its companion post adds PR size +51.3% and a CodeRabbit sample in which AI code carried 1.7× the issues ([Faros review burden](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-code-quality-senior-engineer-review-burden)). DORA's 1,110-engineer Google survey links adoption to higher throughput and higher instability, says author velocity "translate[s] into a significantly increased cognitive load for the reviewer", and advises test automation over review optimisation ([dora.dev](https://dora.dev/insights/balancing-ai-tensions/)); its ROI model coins the "verification tax" ([ajayk.sh summary](https://www.ajayk.sh/blog/devops-still-matters-ai-era-dora-2026/)). Zalando shows the mechanism in production: since Sonnet 4, PRs in the 500–2,000-line buckets grew and AI commit messages reached ~5,000 characters ([Zalando](https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2026/08/agentic-engineering-at-zalando-a-snapshot.html)).

What is *not* in any of these is a CI number. **Faros reports no build or CI metric and does not name CI as a bottleneck**; DORA does not either; the notes record no evidence found for CI wait time, CI spend or flaky-test rate as a function of AI adoption. The CI framing comes from essays: Paul Stack's argument that agents discover failures only after pushing, so verification must move earlier, and Fowler's reply that this is how CI was always meant to work ([Fowler fragments](https://martinfowler.com/fragments/)); SRLabs' observation that generation of code and of security findings collapsed in cost while verification stayed human-limited, so teams "optimize for closing tickets, not risk" ([SRLabs](https://srlabs.de/blog/ai-verification-bottleneck)). Good arguments, not measurements. The one counter-voice is Uncle Bob Martin, who built a strict harness and found better agents made most of it unnecessary ([X](https://x.com/unclebobmartin/status/2098432570887217520)).

So the first thing that saturates is the human reviewer, and the measured response in the wild is to drop review rather than add verification: the +31.3% of PRs merged with no review is Proton's plan appearing as a statistic. The owner's "CI is the bottleneck" is correct in the narrower, Vigil-specific sense that section 5 develops: where review is already a fresh-context model and merge is one human, machine verification is the next thing to saturate. The general claim should read "verification capacity, human first, machine second".

Proton sharpens the stakes. The 404 Media piece rests on nine sources, Slack screenshots and meeting audio: the CTO's manifesto says Proton "needs to get to the point where most code is not reviewed by humans but by AI", the CTO put October's AI-written share at 90% (an in-house tracker, method undescribed), and CEO Yen rejected a pre-release pentest as "loser thinking" and wrote that developers would "write specs for tests that AI codes and runs" ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/manifestos-fear-and-loser-thinking-inside-protons-ai-reckoning/)). No QA process is documented beyond that sentence, and the notes found no Proton engineering post on verifying AI code. The measured state of that division of labour is modest: given a human-written issue, the best agent produces a valid fail-to-pass test on **65.3%** of Multi-SWT-Bench-Lite instances (82.5% Python, 52.5% C++), and the benchmark does not score tautology or leakage ([arXiv 2609.34752](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.34752)); Anthropic's property-testing agent produced 56% valid bug reports raw, 86% after rubric ranking, at about an hour of expert review per accepted bug ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/property-based-testing)). Specs-to-tests works as a *veto* oracle written by someone other than the implementer and checked by execution, which is Vigil's hidden-test rule; as the sole gate with one lineage writing code, tests and review, the evidence is against it.

## 2. Warp's benchmark: copy the matrix and the cadence, not the judge

Warp's Factory Benchmarks have three parts: a task set "curated from your team's prior runs or built from scratch", a set of factory configurations varying model or harness, and a set of scorers; every task runs against every configuration and each run is scored on each dimension ([Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/warp-factory-benchmarks)). Replay works because Warp stores prompts, conversations and pre/post git state and restarts a run from its initial state. Results are Pareto charts (best for cost, for quality, overall), repetitions are a knob ("more repetitions cost more but reduce variance"), and the output is a classifier router written as code that sends simple frontend tasks to the model that won that task type. WarpBench ran **30 tasks × 5 models in 3 h 46 min for $2,130.57**, about $14 per task-run, and moved the default from Opus 5 to Grok 4.6 then GPT-5.6 Sol for a reported **~63% cost-per-PR cut** with merge rate flat ([AlphaSignal](https://alphasignal.ai/news/warp-s-warpbench-slashes-coding-agent-costs-by-63-using-your-own-codebase)). In factory-as-code each suite is `benchmarks/<slug>/suite.yaml` with an `agent` flag marking the single variable under test, tasks as Markdown files, and scorers with `passingScore` and `samplingRate` ([Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/software-factory-as-code)). The cadence rule is explicit: run on model release or when prompts, skills or context change, "not automatically".

Borrow four things: the suite structure with one variable under test; the Pareto view, which item 5 already groups repo × tier × backend; routing rules as code derived from per-shape winners, which is `routing.yaml` with evidence behind it; and the cadence, since the eval is the most expensive thing the factory runs. Do not borrow three. Warp's quality axis is an LLM judge; Databricks dropped its judge because it "rewards sounding right over being right" and scores by held-out tests patched in at checkpoint time ([Databricks](https://www.databricks.com/blog/benchmarking-coding-agents-databricks-multi-million-line-codebase)). Warp publishes **no repetition count, no confidence intervals and no held-out split**; its 69%→87% "task compliance" and $80→$30 come from 30 tasks with a chart that reads $81→$19, so treat them as directional. And its replays "assume future tasks resemble past ones", with production A/B still roadmap.

Task-set construction with evidence behind it is Databricks plus SWE-smith plus SWE-rebench. Databricks mined recent merged PRs, **excluded bot, service-account and fully AI-generated changes**, had humans review each candidate, and rewrote the PR description as an outcome-focused prompt with the solution rationale removed because it "makes the task too easy"; agents then walked git history and recovered the merged fix, so the working copy is now cut off from the repository per run ([Databricks](https://www.databricks.com/blog/benchmarking-coding-agents-databricks-multi-million-line-codebase)). SWE-smith synthesises bugs by five strategies, keeps only patches that break passing tests, yields **50.1%** overall, costs ~$0.72 per repo environment and 2.5¢ per issue text, and drops candidates whose tests exceed two minutes ([SWE-smith](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.21798)). SWE-rebench date-tags tasks against model release dates and reports pass@5 with SEM over five full runs ([SWE-rebench](https://swe-rebench.com/about)). The reason for the date column: Sonnet 3.5/3.7 located all ground-truth files from issue text alone on 65%/63% of SWE-bench Verified but 12% on unseen repos ([arXiv 2512.10218](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.10218v2)); the reason to reword specs: chat-style rephrasing alone dropped success **20–40% relative** ([arXiv 2510.08996](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08996v4)).

Variance is the pitfall the build list underestimates. On 500 tasks the same model and scaffold moved **2.2–6.0 points** across ten runs, and detecting a 2-point difference at 80% power needs about **9 runs per arm** at median variance; a 5-point difference needs 2 ([DEV summary of arXiv 2602.07150](https://dev.to/devinios/same-model-same-scaffold-10-runs-agent-benchmark-scores-still-move-by-up-to-6-points-gmb)). A 20-task set has binomial noise of roughly ±10–15 points per run, so Phase C's "20 tasks each, one number" cannot distinguish ask-gate on from off unless the effect is enormous. Anthropic's guidance says 20–50 tasks suffice *initially*, distinguishes pass@k from pass^k (a 90% agent has pass^10 ≈ 35%), and trusts no score "until someone digs into the details and reads some transcripts" ([summary of Anthropic's post](https://gist.github.com/AnthonyAlcaraz/2f7390a78f6d05beba0c109b1a039523)). The only documented hard gate on prompt changes is promptfoo's: deterministic checks at 100%, judge rubrics under a separate threshold, `--repeat 3`, a path-filtered trigger on `prompts/**` ([Berton](https://lucaberton.com/blog/llm-regression-testing-ci-promptfoo/)); no vendor is on record blocking a prompt PR on a held-out delta, and Cursor's practice is offline evals plus online A/B with one recorded regression of −30% when reasoning traces were dropped ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/codex-model-harness)). Item 14's `template-delta` gate is ahead of published practice and needs a defined tolerance, not "no regression".

## 3. Frontier verification techniques, ranked by evidence and fit

The ranking below weighs measured effect, independence from the worker, and cost on a Mac mini plus a two-GPU tower under a $2/task cap.

**Execution-grounded review (rank 1: strongest evidence, lowest cost).** Groundability audited 411 execution-labelled agent traces with six reviewers from Llama-3.1-8B to Qwen3-235B. With decisive execution evidence **Llama-8B reached 0.98 catch / 0.00 over-rejection** and GPT-OSS-120B 1.00/0.00; with structured-but-unverified evidence the same reviewers sat at 0.28–0.70 catch with up to 0.75 over-rejection, and size did not predict quality. Patch-only review let Llama-8B accept all nine real omission defects because "a diff cannot show what is missing". The design rule: spend on independent checks before reviewer capacity, gate mechanically where evidence is decisive, send only unresolved cases to a reviewer with an abstain option ([arXiv 2610.01023](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.01023)). Vigil's reviewer sees the diff; it should also see the verifier's JUnit diff and hidden-test categories, which are already host-side. Cross-model review is asymmetric: Claude reviewing Codex drafts +18.1 pp, Codex reviewing Claude drafts **−8.6 pp with an 11.2% regression rate**, mostly by rewriting working code ([arXiv 2607.21656](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.21656v1)); Vigil's findings-not-rewrites contract already avoids that. Single review tools match 20–32% of human findings and the union of four 41.5% ([c-CRAB](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.23448v2)), so at rung 1 measure union catch, not agreement. No study injects SAST findings into an LLM reviewer and measures the change; no evidence found.

**Replay-eval rigor (rank 2).** Section 2: repeats, date tags, no judge, and gold-patch gating of every generated test.

**Verifier throughput: cache, flake policy, selection (rank 3).** This is where "CI is the bottleneck" becomes true inside Vigil. The base run is identical for every attempt on a base SHA and is cacheable by (base SHA, `test_cmd`, dev-image digest); bookkeeping, not weakening. Flakes need a policy before any "red = reject" rule: Google measured **1.5% of executions flaky and 40.5% of targets that both passed and failed as flaky** ([Google TAP](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/45861.pdf)); Atlassian attributes 15–21% of master failures to flakes ([TestDino compilation](https://testdino.com/blog/flaky-test-benchmark)). For selection, the Python result is NAMERTS: name-based selection over 500 commits in 10 projects **skips 69.9% of test files, cuts time 45.6%, is safe on 99.6% of commits, at ~10% overhead** ([arXiv 2605.25356](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25356v1)); Meta runs a third of dependent tests and catches >99.9% of regressions ([Meta](https://engineering.fb.com/2018/11/21/developer-tools/predictive-test-selection/)). Both rely on a later full run; Vigil's shape is selection for before/after plus the nightly full run it already has. Two caveats: NAMERTS measures human commits, and agent change patterns may degrade selection (argued in a blog, no data verified, [ThinhDA](https://thinhdanggroup.github.io/test-selection-under-agent-load/)); and selection changes the verdict's coverage, so it goes behind a flag and is measured on the replay pool first.

**Hackability audit and test admission (rank 4).** The audit protocol generates K deliberately wrong patches over three rounds using failure logs; a task whose tests pass any is hackable. One round found 18.4% hackable, three rounds **28.5%**, and models score +14.1 pp on hackable tasks. Its guards: run every generated test against the gold patch before trusting a judge (61.9% of "decisive" augmentations failed on gold), keep a retry step (repairs fell 9/11 → 3/11 without it), and re-verify judge verdicts by execution ([arXiv 2606.16062](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.16062)). SWE-Bench Pro Verified lists the leakage channels (local FS, git history, network, task metadata; a baseline agent read local answer files in 103 of 731 tasks) and the fixes Vigil mostly has ([arXiv 2609.08149](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.08149v1)). Item 4's single wrong patch is one round; the data say K>1 with log-guided retries. PatchDiff is the complement for the replay pool: differential tests flagged **29.6%** of plausible SWE-bench patches as behaviourally different from the oracle, 82.7% of them uncaught by the full developer suite, and 66.2% of suspicious patches were unjudgeable because the issue under-specified behaviour ([arXiv 2503.15223](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15223v1)), the strongest argument yet for the ask-gate's `done_when` test.

**Best-of-n with the host verifier choosing (rank 5: large effect, fits only the local backend).** DeepSWE's 32B model goes from **42.2% pass@1 to 59% Best@16** with a hybrid execution+LLM verifier, Best@8 at 57.9% and pass@16 at 71% as the ceiling, so returns flatten past ~8 ([Together](https://www.together.ai/blog/deepswe)). SWE-Verifier is the cheap variant: a Qwen3.5-4B step-level verifier buys +1.7 pp at **1.09–1.13× cost** by checking under 4% of steps, versus 3.5–4.2× for trajectory-level best-of-3 ([arXiv 2610.30284](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.30284)). Two cautions: part of any gain can be the verifier being gameable (the 14.1 pp above), so a best-of-n result against Vigil's hidden tests is trusted only after the hackability audit; and at hosted prices n=8 breaks the $2/task cap, so this is a tower technique. No published result samples n local candidates and selects by a host verifier; no evidence found.

**Mutation-guided tests (rank 6: quality signal, not gate).** Meta's ACH generated 571 mutant-killing tests, **49% of which added no line coverage** and would have been discarded by a coverage criterion, at 73–90% engineer acceptance ([arXiv 2501.12862](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12862v1)); MUTGEN's motivating case is 100% coverage with a 4% mutation score ([arXiv 2506.02954](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02954)). The constraint is the replication study: mutation scores are meaningful "when comparing across models" in regression settings where the code is assumed correct, and unreliable when the code may be buggy ([arXiv 2607.22880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22880)). So mutation score on changed lines belongs in the scoreboard's quality column, never as a gate. No paper uses mutation score as an RL or best-of-n reward; no evidence found.

**Property-based tests (rank 7: for the hidden-test writer, not the worker).** PBT-Bench puts Hypothesis-scaffolded bug recall at **42.1–83.4%** and names the failure modes: `assume()` misuse and wrong strategy ranges are ~62% of PBT-mode failures, incorrect assertions 59% of baseline failures ([PBT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.15229)), a ready checklist for the hidden-test session. Forcing the *worker* to write tests is measured as waste: no outcome change, +19.8% output tokens, ~25 prints per 5 assertions; write one minimal regression test and run the smallest slice ([arXiv 2602.07900](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.07900)).

**Security checks in a no-egress container (rank 8).** Semgrep Assistant agrees with researchers on 96% of true positives but 41% of false positives ([Semgrep](https://semgrep.dev/blog/2025/building-an-appsec-ai-that-security-researchers-agree-with-96-of-the-time/)); Anthropic's Mythos validated >90% of an assessed subset but is not available ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/05/26/anthropic-project-glasswing-update/)). The feasible stack is Semgrep OSS and ruff/bandit as findings, plus SRLabs' **one negative test for any auth or payments change** ([SRLabs](https://srlabs.de/blog/ai-verification-bottleneck)), which maps onto routing rule 2.

**Warm pools versus snapshots (rank 9: snapshots are unavailable where Vigil runs).** Firecracker snapshot restore is ~28 ms against ~1.1 s cold boot but needs Linux with KVM ([dev.to](https://dev.to/adwitiya/how-i-built-sandboxes-that-boot-in-28ms-using-firecracker-snapshots-i0k)); Docker Desktop for Mac has an open "checkpoint not working" issue since the 1.13 era ([docker/for-mac #1059](https://github.com/docker/for-mac/issues/1059)). On the Mac mini the levers are a pre-started pool of session containers (the cost is clone and install, not the 0.2–0.5 s start), byte-compiled site-packages in the dev image, and the base-run cache. No Apple Silicon measurements exist.

**Formal verification (rank 10: not now).** Vero's best configuration solved 27/43 multi-module tasks and names "specification drift" as a failure mode ([Vero summary](https://codex.danielvaughan.com/2026/08/14/vero-benchmark-formally-verified-software-repositories-coding-agents-codex-cli-proof-synthesis-posttooluse-verification/)); Anthropic notes "verification can be gamed" by rewriting the program to never crash ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/formal-verification)). The lesson transfers (freeze the spec outside the worker's reach); the technique does not fit a Python repo at this budget.

## 4. One executor contract over five CLIs and a local model

Eleven harnesses converge on seven subsystems, with SKILL.md in 9/11 and MCP in 8/11, but their permission models and output schemas do not transfer ([Harness Engineering](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.00006v1)). The nearest prior art to "packet in, structured result out" is the Agent Client Protocol as OpenHands uses it: session create, prompt, streamed updates, permission requests, turn result, each agent owning its LLM calls and credentials ([OpenHands](https://openhands.dev/blog/use-any-coding-agent-in-openhands-with-acp)). Vigil's contract keeps that shape and adds the gateway token on stdin, cost from gateway metering, and the `vigil_result` line. Since every CLI's JSON differs and OpenCode's is undocumented, a version-pinned per-CLI adapter is unavoidable.

The deferred line "Codex, Cursor headless, OpenCode" becomes OpenCode, then Codex, with Cursor and Gemini dropped until either accepts a proxy URL. Permission normalisation is cleanest at the container boundary (no egress, uid 10001, read-only mounts) with each CLI at its most permissive non-interactive mode; Codex's `external-sandbox` is the precedent. Expect four swap failures: hooks not firing (no non-Claude CLI has a PreToolUse analogue), tool-name drift in result parsing, wire-API mismatch for local backends, and silently proposed-not-applied edits (Cursor without `--force`, Codex's default read-only sandbox).

Two facts matter for the #121 follow-up. Claude Code computes cost "locally from token counts at list price, unless a `modelPricing` table is in effect", a managed-settings-only field from 2.1.242 with a `multiplier` and per-model `overrides` ([Manage costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs)); and the price `/model` shows "is a display label only" ([Model configuration](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/model-config)). So instead of dropping `--max-budget-usd` for local backends, Vigil can set `modelPricing` for the local model IDs in `managed-settings.json` (zero, or a GPU-second-derived rate) and keep the cap as a runaway brake. How an ID absent from the built-in table is priced is unverified; test it first. The gateway remains the cost of record either way, since the client cap overshoots and only Claude Code reports a dollar figure.

**Where the local model fits.** A Q4 Qwen3.6-35B-A3B is ~22–24 GB of weights, so on two 16 GB cards it runs layer-split; the one two-card measurement is 91–95 tok/s split versus 58 single-GPU ([Strata #3294](https://github.com/Niko1221/Strata/issues/3294)), and CUDA 13.2 produced gibberish where 12.8 worked ([aminrj](https://aminrj.com/posts/llamacpp-qwen36-35b/)). MTP speculative decoding roughly doubles single-stream decode but loses under concurrency (28.3 vs 13.1 tok/s at one stream, 29.9 vs 41.5 at four), so a worker profile (MTP on, `--parallel 1–2`) and a reviewer-panel profile (MTP off, `--parallel 4–8`) are two `llama-server` launches that will not both fit beside a 22 GB model ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/mtp-llama-cpp-a-look-at-qwen3-6-27b/370298)). On task shape, frontier agents sit near 50% on SWE-bench Pro's 107-line, 4.1-file tasks and **42% of smaller-model failures are tool-use inefficiency** ([Morph](https://morphllm.com/swe-bench-pro)); the only Qwen3.6-35B-A3B coding number is a 22-task battery at 21/21 ([SD Times](https://sdtimes.com/sdt_dev/frontier-models-write-the-code-local-models-should-review-it/)), and a qwen3-coder:30b agent backend scored 22.8 against Claude's 89.4 on a 27-task tool-use replay with malformed tool calls in 26% of tasks ([Apostolov](https://dev.to/sikamikanikobg/local-llm-vs-claude-benchmarking-qwen3-coder30b-as-a-production-agent-backend-482b)). No published team shows a ≤35B local model winning any own-repo shape against a frontier model under hidden tests; no evidence found. Harness matters as much as model at this size: Devstral-2 scored 63.5% under one scaffold and 34.9% under another ([DEV summary of arXiv 2602.07150](https://dev.to/devinios/same-model-same-scaffold-10-runs-agent-benchmark-scores-still-move-by-up-to-6-points-gmb)). The roles the evidence supports, in order: grounded reviewer with the verifier's JUnit diff (Groundability's 32B proxy reached 0.96/0.09 with evidence), best-of-n candidate generator for single-file shapes with the host verifier selecting, and only then a solo worker on tests-from-spec and docs shapes. Open-weight models found 53–72% of planted vulnerabilities against 89–96% commercial, rising to 96.9% cross-referenced with static analysis, and a deliberately impossible task "passed" in six of eight configurations in the same author's rig ([SD Times](https://sdtimes.com/sdt_dev/frontier-models-write-the-code-local-models-should-review-it/)), so the local reviewer gets Semgrep output and every replay carries a must-fail canary.

## 5. What Vigil does well, and what it is not yet considering

Vigil's trust path is better evidenced than any production system in the notes. Hidden tests in a fresh single-commit checkout, a worker that never grades itself, an abort outcome, a lexicographic reward with no judge, egress denial and a human merge each map onto a measured failure they prevent, and Proton is the control case: one lineage writing code, tests and review, with the pentest gate removed. The earlier reports established that; this one adds that execution evidence, not reviewer size, makes review reliable, which makes the host-side verifier the asset to build around rather than a cost centre.

What it is not considering, thinking as someone trying to push this frontier rather than defend the design. **The eval is not powered.** A 20-task single-run arm is noise, and at Databricks' ~$1.3–2.1 per task-run ([Databricks](https://www.databricks.com/blog/benchmarking-coding-agents-databricks-multi-million-line-codebase)) 20 tasks × 3 repeats × 2 arms is ~$240, more than the $200 month, for one of Phase C's three experiments. The honest plan is one hosted arm per month, ask-gate first, with the other two on the local backend once it exists. **The verifier is the factory's real CI bottleneck and nothing in the list addresses its cost.** Three full runs per attempt, no base cache, no flake policy, no selection; add retries, chains re-verified at land, rebase-on-land and any best-of-n, and verifier wall-time multiplies while the Mac mini runs perhaps three or four sessions in an 8–12 GB Docker VM ([OneUptime](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-01-16-docker-mac-apple-silicon/markdown)). The owner's intuition is right here even though the public data do not show it. **Flakes have no policy**; at Google's rates a hard reject on one red run rejects good work daily, and quarantine state is a per-repo table, not a prompt. **The reviewer is under-informed**; it sees the diff and not the verifier's evidence, which Groundability says is the difference between 0.28 and 0.98 catch for a small model, and between a local reviewer that can graduate and one that cannot. **The human is the next bottleneck.** Faros's +441% is per human reviewer and Vigil has one; every job PR crosses the owner, the auto-land rung (V5) is the only item that changes that, and the planted-defect catalog it depends on is scheduled nowhere in Phases A–C. Zalando's 33% auto-approved low-risk PRs at 20–40% lower lead time, with engineers reshaping PRs to hit the tier ([Zalando](https://engineering.zalando.com/posts/2026/08/agentic-engineering-at-zalando-a-snapshot.html)), is both the prize and the Goodhart warning for item 10's 100-line cap. **Specs are under-tested**: PatchDiff's 66.2% "unjudgeable" means the ask-gate is a correctness instrument, and its A/B should measure the unjudgeable rate, not only retries. **The local model plan is aspirational**: the tower cannot tune its model (prior report), no evidence shows it winning a shape as a worker, and its best-evidenced roles need the verifier's evidence and a best-of-n loop that do not exist yet. **Harness and model are coupled**: Cursor lost 30% when a harness change dropped reasoning traces and Databricks saw >2× cost at equal quality across harnesses, so a backend row in `routing.yaml` is really a (model, harness, prompt) triple and the scoreboard should key on it. **Security verification is tests only**; the cheap additions are Semgrep OSS as findings and a negative test for money and auth paths.

## Conclusion: the five findings that most change what Vigil does next

1. **Execution evidence, not a bigger reviewer, is the lever.** With the verifier's output in the packet an 8B model reviews at 0.98 catch / 0.00 over-rejection; without it every size sits at 0.28–0.70 ([Groundability](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.01023)). This is a packet change, it is what lets the local reviewer graduate, and it is not in the build list.
2. **Phase C as written measures noise and overspends.** A 20-task single run carries ±10–15 points; a 2-point effect needs ~9 runs per arm on 500 tasks ([arXiv 2602.07150 summary](https://dev.to/devinios/same-model-same-scaffold-10-runs-agent-benchmark-scores-still-move-by-up-to-6-points-gmb)), and three repeated hosted arms cost more than a month's $200. One hosted arm per month, repeats, date tags, no judge, transcripts read.
3. **The CI bottleneck is real inside Vigil even though the public data do not show it.** Faros and DORA measure review and defects, not CI ([Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-acceleration-whiplash-takeaways)); but three uncached runs per attempt, no flake policy and no selection will cap the factory before the budget does. Base-run cache, quarantine, and NAMERTS-style selection with a nightly full run keep the verdict's coverage at 99.6% commit safety ([NAMERTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.25356v1)).
4. **One wrong patch is not an audit.** Hackability rose from 18.4% to 28.5% between one and three rounds, and 61.9% of judge-endorsed generated tests failed on the gold patch ([arXiv 2606.16062](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.16062)); item 4 needs K rounds and execution gating of every generated test, or the hidden set admits what it should veto.
5. **The local model's first job is candidate generation and grounded review, not solo work.** A 32B model gains +16.8 pp when a host verifier selects among 16 samples ([Together](https://www.together.ai/blog/deepswe)), small models lose 42% of tasks to tool-use inefficiency ([Morph](https://morphllm.com/swe-bench-pro)), and no one has shown a ≤35B model winning an own-repo shape under hidden tests. The executor contract should ship with OpenCode and Codex; Cursor, Gemini and Amp cannot run keyless.
