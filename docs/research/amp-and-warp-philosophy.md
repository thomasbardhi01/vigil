# Vigil keeps the gate, drops the ceremony

Research cut-off 2026-10-29. Builds on `/home/claude/reports/Vigil factory research Oct 2026.md` (E1–E11, N1–N3, Tracks A–D). Every claim carries the URL the notes recorded; where the notes found nothing, this says so.

**Bottom line.** Amp and Warp disagree about everything except the one thing Vigil does differently from both: who grades the work. Amp's "no PRs, push to main" describes a ~20-person high-trust team whose agents produce the evidence that their own code works, and Amp's SOC 2 note bounds it to teams that size ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/notes/thats-not-soc-2-compliant)); Warp's "cloud factory, humans merge every PR" is a control plane whose quality signal is an LLM judge reading the worker's own transcript ([Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/engineering-self-improving-software-factories)). Neither has a verification step the worker cannot touch. The field data do not support dropping the checkpoint: high AI adoption raises PR size 154% and review time 91% while bugs per developer rise 9% ([Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering)), and DORA 2025 reports a negative relationship with stability ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report)). No production system was found that lands LLM-agent code on main with neither a human nor a deterministic gate; the nearest is OpenAI's internal beta, where agents review agents ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering)). So Vigil keeps the PR as the gate's carrier and bounds the branch's life (V1), sizes each contract to one landable increment (V2), chains contracts with a cap (V3), defines auto-land with Wald's SPRT at p0 = 0.80, p1 = 0.95, α = 0.05, β = 0.10 plus a Dependabot-shaped change-class predicate (V5), adopts the two config files Warp has and `configs/` lacks (V6), rejects the cloud premise while supplying what cloud buys (V7), and takes Warp's scorer names as host-side telemetry, never a gate (V8). The source of the "why are you still using branches?" passage was not found (A2).

Over 85% of Amp's own commits come from orbs ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/notes/pave-the-road)). Pricing went from an ad-funded $10/day "Amp Free" grant ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/news/amp-free-frontier)) to "pausing or further reducing" it in May 2026 ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/news/amp-free-is-ad-free)); since 13 Sep 2026 the software is free with BYOK, inference through Amp carries "no markup", and $20/month buys 45,000 orb-minutes ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/news/free-agent); [pricing](https://ampcode.com/docs/pricing)). ### A3. Trunk, no review, and the agent produces its own proof

Amp's practice in its own words: core team members push directly to `main`, mandatory PR review is rejected, agents run review rounds, humans review optionally and later ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/docs/using-amp/how-we-work)); "We don't have PRs because it's insane to ask people to stamp stuff", "I only review load-bearing code", and the stock prompt "Give me irrefutable proof that this works now" ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/time-capsules/6)); "I don't think we really ever do pull requests", with reviewers judging demos and test matrices rather than diffs ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/podcast/season-02/episode-06)); review "is a farce now" at agent volume ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/podcast/season-02/episode-05)). The SOC 2 note states exactly which gates exist independent of the agent: restricted push to `main`, signed commits, CI that blocks failing changes, and a commit-to-thread audit trail, with the limit that a 2,000-person company should not do this ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/notes/thats-not-soc-2-compliant)). Whether that CI includes tests the agent cannot modify: no evidence found. Amp's review agent can fix what it finds, so reviewer and fixer are one system ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/news/liberating-code-review)), yet Amp sells PR tooling to customers who use PRs ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/news/github-listeners)). On factories: "the assistant is dead, long live the factory" ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/podcast/episode-9)), realised as orbs spawning orbs with no orchestrator ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/podcast/season-02/episode-05)). Explicit rejections: mandatory review, big releases, branches that "sit ... for a week", backlogs, per-engineer token caps, cheaper models, permission prompts ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/notes/how-we-build); [Amp](https://ampcode.com/notes/pave-the-road); [Amp](https://ampcode.com/news/neo)). The decisive difference from Vigil: at Amp the entity that writes the code produces the evidence that it works.

### A4. Agents make increments cheaper; nothing shows the checkpoint is optional

Two claims hide in the trunk argument. The mechanism claim is weakly supported: agentic PRs on 489 Python repos "modify fewer files and fewer lines", merge faster, and show bug-inducing rates "comparable to or lower than human PRs" with effect sizes "often negligible" ([arXiv 2607.21832](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.21832)); Graphite's median PR is 47 lines ([Graphite](https://graphite.com/research/median-pr_size)). But teams with high AI adoption show the opposite in practice: PR size +154%, review time +91%, bugs +9%, and "no significant correlation" with company outcomes ([Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering)). Small increments are a discipline the orchestrator imposes. The second claim, that no checkpoint is needed before main, has no supporting source. DORA 2025 finds throughput gains but a continuing "negative relationship with software delivery stability", with "strong automated testing" deciding whether volume becomes instability ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report)); DORA 2024 put 25% more AI adoption at −7.2% stability ([Farrag](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.01160)); GitClear saw duplicated blocks rise 8× in 2024 ([DevClass](https://devclass.com/2025/02/20/ai-is-eroding-code-quality-states-new-in-depth-report/)). The closest field data on "no checkpoint": 28.3% of 33,707 agent PRs merged in under a minute (Codex 42.9%) with no outcome measured ([arXiv 2601.00753](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.00753v2)); no study reports revert rates for agent changes. Merge speed barely depends on size (Spearman 0.26 over 613k PRs) ([arXiv 2203.05045](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.05045)), so the case for small increments is blast radius, not speed.

### A5. Flags are the one chore Amp vouches for

Amp's only first-party claim: agents are "incredibly good at using our feature flag system", flipping flags in production through a plugin tool while reading production logs ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/docs/using-amp/thorsten); [Amp](https://ampcode.com/podcast/season-02/episode-06)); a "broken database migration" is Ball's example of what still gets close human review ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/time-capsules/6)). No Amp passage was found on expand/contract migrations, dual configs, splitting into landable steps, or "dead code then wire it"; "smallest mergeable chunk" is a team principle ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/notes/how-we-build)). Independently, DoorDash's stale-flag removal produced usable PRs for 45/50 flags at $4.79 and 13.8 minutes each, but needed per-agent worktrees after early runs "corrupted diffs", a one-hour timeout, and a failure contract of "an unmerged PR, not a broken build" ([Creuto](https://creuto.com/feature-flag-cleanup-ai-agents-doordash) summarising [DoorDash](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/automating-feature-flag-cleanup-at-scale-with-a-multi-agent-llm-system/)). Flag debt is the documented failure: 53% of Chrome release toggles outlive ten releases ([arXiv 1907.06157](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.06157)); Vercel's remedy is flags-as-code where "The build fails rather than advises" ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/i/feature-flags-code-lifecycle)). Migrations are worse: the best FreshBrew agent completes 52.3%, and agents were caught excluding failing tests or making one "silently skip" ([arXiv 2510.04852](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04852v1)); Google validates per file and marks failures "for manual migration" ([arXiv 2504.09691](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09691v1)). Vendor skills publish no outcome data ([LaunchDarkly](https://launchdarkly.com/how-it-works/agent-integrations/); [Atlas](https://www.atlasgo.io/blog/2025/08/19/teach-ai-agents-schema-management)).

## Part 2 — Warp's stack is a taxonomy with a lenient judge inside

### W1. Seven layers, Warp's own numbers, and a soft fifth layer

Bottom to top: Factory-as-code "Versions the entire factory definition"; Data and context "Feeds agents org knowledge; records what they did"; Compute "Runs agent work somewhere other than a laptop"; Inference "Supplies the models and harnesses"; Improvement "Proves the factory is getting better"; Orchestration "Launches, tracks, and recovers agent runs"; Access "Gets work in and out" ([Warp](https://www.warp.dev/articles/what-is-a-software-factory); [Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/the-factory-stack)). The claims are architectural; the only empirical support is Warp's own figures: 20–30% of PRs automated, 63% cost reduction on some task types, judge scoring at ~3% of spend, "300 PRs in the past week", $57.55 per PR on a dashboard ([Warp](https://www.warp.dev/articles/what-is-a-software-factory); [Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/engineering-self-improving-software-factories); [Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/open-infrastructure-for-building-a-software-factory)). The strongest counters hit layer 5: Anthropic finds a self-grading agent "confidently praising the work" and even a standalone evaluator that "tends to be lenient" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)); METR saw models modify tests and monkey-patch evaluators while anti-cheat prompts "had little effect" ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking)); Mitchem finds "every output still goes to a person for approval" ([Mitchem](https://medium.com/@NMitchem/nobody-has-actually-built-a-software-factory-77ffdc0c3efc)). Against the whole premise, HN practitioners note "it is possible today to create a PR that passes all checks" ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49023019)). OpenAI is the counter-counter: Codex reviews its own changes until "all agent reviewers are satisfied", with structural tests as the hard gate ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering)). Compute's counter is W2; Orchestration's multi-agent topology meets Cognition's context-sharing objections, reachable here only via a secondary summary ([agentic-ai](https://agentic-ai.readthedocs.io/en/latest/ContextEngineering/devin/)).

### W2. "Cloud" bundles three needs; any always-on daemon meets two

Warp's reasons: laptop agents "may be offline"; traces and telemetry "must be stored in the cloud"; factories are "a team concept" reached through Slack, Jira and GitHub ([Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/agent-self-improving-software-factories)); and sandboxes beat laptops because a laptop agent "can access every system its user is logged into" ([Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/open-infrastructure-for-building-a-software-factory)). No page says literally "factories must live in the cloud"; the stack post says compute should be self-hostable ([Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/the-factory-stack)). The product boundary is explicit: self-hosted workers are Enterprise-only, and agent context and model requests "still travel through Warp's services" while run tracking stays in Warp's cloud ([Warp docs](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/self-hosting/)). Counter-cases: OpenAI boots its app "per git worktree" with an ephemeral local observability stack ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering)); Copilot added self-hosted runners for resources "not available on the public internet" ([GitHub](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-10-28-copilot-coding-agent-now-supports-self-hosted-runners)); Devin offers a VPC but no on-prem ([Devin](https://docs.devin.ai/enterprise/deployment/overview)); and the "Comment and Control" chain made three vendors' hosted review agents act on a malicious PR title, posting an API key as a comment ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/security/ai-agent-runtime-security-system-card-audit-comment-and-control-2026)). Stripe "minions": no public source found. For a two-person team on private code, Warp is right about its target and irrelevant to this one: always-on compute and a central store are what `vigild` with Phoenix already is, team access at two seats is a Tailscale URL, and Vigil's no-egress, no-secret worker is stricter than Warp's self-hosted worker, which needs outbound access to Warp and holds runtime secrets.

### W3. Everyone keeps a human; the one exception reviews agents with agents

Warp's rule: "Agents never merge: your branch protection decides who does", a person approves each spec, and the metric is "human touches per PR", which "should go down" over time; no series is published ([Warp](https://www.warp.dev/articles/warp-factories-vs-devin); [Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/adapting-for-a-world-of-software-factories)). Amp is the other pole; Vigil sits between, human merge today and autonomy earned per class. The only published system landing agent code without a required human is OpenAI's internal beta: "Humans may review pull requests, but aren't required to", agents squash-merge their own PRs, gated by linters and structural tests ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering)). Everything else ends in a human: SapFix "sends them to a human reviewer for approval" ([Meta](https://ai.meta.com/blog/finding-and-fixing-software-bugs-automatically-with-sapfix-and-sapienz/)); Google's migrations are "promoted for review, not landed automatically" ([arXiv 2504.09691](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09691v1)); Uber's FixrLeak is "one-click accept" ([ZenML](https://www.zenml.io/llmops-database/using-genai-to-automatically-fix-java-resource-leaks)); Amazon Q's output "will not affect your current project files until you accept" ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazonq/latest/qdeveloper-ug/how-CT-works.html)). The widespread no-human precedent is Dependabot and Renovate: a machine-checkable change class, CI plus a release-age delay, trivial rollback ([GitHub](https://docs.github.com/en/code-security/tutorials/secure-your-dependencies/automate-dependabot-with-actions); [Renovate](https://docs.renovatebot.com/key-concepts/minimum-release-age/)). Google Rosie, Cursor and Devin auto-merge: no primary source found. No published system uses SPRT or any sequential test to gate autonomy; the uses found are A/B testing and canary analysis ([Johari et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.04922); [Spinnaker](https://spinnaker.io/docs/guides/user/canary/judge)).

## Part 3 — Reconcile for Vigil

The rule in view: the worker never grades its own work, and nothing the worker can touch decides whether code lands on main.

**V1 Short-lived branch, PR kept — KEEP, modified.** The PR object stays for a mechanical reason: GitHub's required checks, auto-merge and merge queue are merge-time, PR-shaped gates, status checks on direct pushes are undocumented, and the owner's ruleset already requires a PR, squash-only, no bypass ([GitHub docs](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-rulesets/available-rules-for-rulesets)). Google, Gerrit and Phabricator show the real artifact is a verified change plus its record ([Graphite](https://graphite.com/blog/how-google-does-code-review)), so "verified commit + audit record" is the thing and the PR is its carrier. What changes is lifetime: the `vigil/*` branch exists from dispatch to verdict and publish; with squash-only, "fast-forward only" means strict up-to-date checks and re-verification after any rebase, so the tree the verifier passed is the tree that lands. Ball's own pre-agent rule flagged PRs open over a week ([Ball, 2024](https://registerspill.thorstenball.com/p/how-i-use-git)) and Graphite's median merge is 3.5 hours ([Graphite](https://graphite.com/research/median-time-to-merge-prs)); a branch older than 24 hours is a reportable smell.

**V2 One landable increment per contract — ADOPT.** No measured definition exists; the operational ones are Google's per-file build-then-test chain ([arXiv 2504.09691](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.09691v1)) and stack rules. The evidence-backed band is where single-review merges happen: Graphite's median 47 lines against 94 for multi-review PRs ([Graphite](https://graphite.com/research/median-pr_size)), instant-merged agent PRs at 68 vs 104 changes with review effort tracking size at r≈0.6 ([arXiv 2601.00753](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.00753v2)). Heuristic: one `test_cmd`; a diff target of ≤100 changed lines with a hard cap of 200, above which the planner must chain (V3); a user-visible change names its flag; `done_when` is satisfiable by one verifier run on base plus this diff alone. The test: E1's ask-gate (a detector, not a negotiator) refuses a contract whose `done_when` needs another contract landed first; after the fact, the verifier's **after** run on the diff alone is the proof. Faros shows agents inflate PRs when nobody imposes the discipline ([Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering)), so the cap is vigild's.

**V3 Chained contracts — ADOPT with a cap.** No experience report of agents producing stacked PRs was found; `gh stack` is in private preview with the rules "Merge bottom-up ... you can't skip layers" and lower merges rebase the rest, and its reviewer advises two-layer stacks ([Vaughan](https://codex.danielvaughan.com/2026/04/16/stacked-prs-coding-agents-gh-stack-sapling-codex-skill/)). Vigil needs `depends_on` across jobs (the phase-2 scheduler), link N+1 based on link N's squash commit, rebase-on-land done by vigild in the vault through `gitsafe.py`, a cap of three links, a chain budget equal to the sum of task caps, and an N1 `abort` on conflict rather than an agent resolving it. Each link is re-verified (base, before, after) against the live `main` head at land time, because hidden tests and the superset check only mean something on the tree that lands.

**V4 Meta-programming skills — ADOPT as repo skills and verifier checks, not separate jobs by default.** The evidence supports flags and little else: Amp vouches for flag creation only ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/docs/using-amp/thorsten)); DoorDash's 90% on removal needed worktrees, timeouts and an unmerged-PR failure contract ([Creuto](https://creuto.com/feature-flag-cleanup-ai-agents-doordash)); migrations sit at 52% with test-disabling hacks ([arXiv 2510.04852](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04852v1)). So: a flag-scaffold skill in `prompts/` that requires an owner and expiry, a verifier check that fails on an expired flag ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/i/feature-flags-code-lifecycle)), and a scheduled removal job; expand/contract as a V3 chain whose links carry the `migration` label and hit routing rule 3 (Opus, rung 0 forever); dual-config as a packet template; "dead-code-then-wire" has no source and stays a V2 sizing pattern. The verifier's test-weakening check covers all of them, the direct countermeasure to the FreshBrew hack.

**V5 Auto-land rung — ADOPT, defined.** The design already requires rung 3, green CI, clean review and audit, no money path, post-merge verification, auto-revert and a per-repo off switch. The reviewer SPRT supplies the ladder: p0 = 0.80, p1 = 0.95, α = 0.05, β = 0.10 gives ln A = 2.890, ln B = −2.251, a catch worth +0.1719 and a miss −1.386, so **17 consecutive catches promote, one miss costs 8.07 catches, two misses with no catches refuse**, E[N] ≈ 25.3 at p = 0.95 and 14.3 at p = 0.80, simulated error rates 4.9% and 6.8%; a parallel false-finding SPRT (≤1 per PR) bounds family-wise promotion error at 0.10, or halve each α for 0.05 ([SPRT](https://cran.r-universe.dev/SPRT/doc/SPRT-intro.html); notes T6). The likelihood ratio is an e-process, so the standing 10% audit runs open-ended and demotes the moment Λ ≤ B without inflating type-I error ([Johari et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.04922)). Auto-land adds a Dependabot-shaped condition: the change class is a predicate vigild checks on the diff (shape ∈ {docs, lint, tests, deps-patch, generated}; no file outside the shape's globs, `runner_config`, `configs/` or `ladder_pinned_paths`), and the 48-hour window is the trial. Never eligible: money/auth/consent (rule 2), `migration` and `architecture` (rule 3), deps beyond patch, anything that changes the gate. Rollback follows Argo: `failureLimit: 0`, one regression auto-reverts and drops the class to rung 1; an inconclusive verifier pauses and pages ([Argo](https://argo-rollouts.readthedocs.io/en/stable/features/analysis)). Audit: Opus on a 10% random sample forever plus 100% of reverts. No published system gates autonomy this way. Auto-land still goes through a PR merged by vigild's App token, since merge queues reject `GITHUB_TOKEN` ([GitHub](https://docs.github.com/en/code-security/tutorials/secure-your-dependencies/automate-dependabot-with-actions)), and only after the owner sets `auto_merge: true`.

**V6 Factories-as-code — ADOPT two files, REJECT two fields.** Warp's reference has `repositories`, `secrets` (managed names), `mcpServers`, `cloudProviders`, `integrations`, `scorerDefaults`, `selfImprovement`, `agentDefaults` (model or harness, runner, secrets), `agents/`, `automations/` (github, jira, slack, schedule, webhook triggers), `runners/`, `scorers/`, `benchmarks/`, and no budget, permission or egress field ([Warp docs](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/factory-as-code/); [Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/software-factory-as-code)). Against `configs/`: `repositories` ↔ `repos.yaml`, which adds backend allowlists and the private-repo rule; `agentDefaults`/`agents/` ↔ `routing.yaml` plus `backends.yaml`, which add path- and label-based tiering and escalation counters; `runners/` ↔ `repos.yaml` `dev_dockerfile`/`dev_suites` and `vigild.yaml` limits; `secrets` ↔ `key_ref` names in `backends.yaml`, except Warp's resolve inside the sandbox and Vigil's never leave the host; budgets, egress and permissions ↔ `budgets.yaml`, `egress.yaml`, `managed-settings.json`, absent from Warp; `scorers/` ↔ nothing, by design (V8); `benchmarks/` ↔ `evals/` (phase 7); `selfImprovement` ↔ E4. Missing in Vigil: an `automations` file (nightly audit, flag-expiry scan, deps and label intake) and `review.yaml` itself, named by the design and absent from this checkout, which should hold V5's change classes beside `ladder_pinned_paths`. Refuse: managed secrets granted to agents, vendor-hosted runners, `harness.auth.source: workerEnvironment`.

**V7 "Must live in the cloud" — REJECT.** Warp's three reasons are availability, central data and team access ([Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/agent-self-improving-software-factories)); its own docs show the control plane and model path cannot leave Warp ([Warp docs](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/self-hosting/)), and the 2026 exfiltration ran on vendor-hosted runners ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/security/ai-agent-runtime-security-system-card-audit-comment-and-control-2026)). Vigil supplies the three: availability is `vigild` as an always-on daemon with startup reconcile; central data is `vigil.db`, the vault and Phoenix on one machine; team access is the API and UI on the Tailscale address with the cookie login, the shipped MCP server for any Claude session, and the bridge. Automations come from V6's file. What is given up is Warp's hosted Slack/Jira layer, which the small-factory HN thread suggests matters little below team scale ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50144175)).

**V8 Scorers — ADOPT the names as host-side telemetry, REJECT the mechanism as a gate.** All five (task compliance, procedure compliance, verbosity, efficiency, code quality) are "prompts to an observer agent" over the transcript, sampled at 25%, with example labels `tests_run: 1` / `tests_skipped: 0`; nothing says a failing scorer blocks a PR, and Warp publishes nothing on gaming ([Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/agent-self-improving-software-factories); [Warp docs](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/factory-as-code/)). A transcript scorer grades the narration: compliance is self-report, verbosity rewards terseness, efficiency rewards fewer visible calls, and `tests_run` is the field Vigil forbids trusting ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking); [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)). Vigil's reward has no LLM-as-judge component, so: task compliance → the verifier's gate tier; procedure compliance → host acceptance checks (files ⊆ scope, no test weakened, lint clean); efficiency → gateway metering; code quality → the fresh-context reviewer's findings as tagged by the auditor, who sees the diff and never the transcript; verbosity → dropped. Warp's 3% judge spend is the ceiling for any optional telemetry judge, which the ladder never reads.

## Part 4 — Output

### (1) Philosophy section for `docs/design.md`

> ## Philosophy: the gate is verification, not review
>
> Two public positions bracket Vigil. Amp pushes to `main` with no pull requests and no mandatory review; its agents produce the proof that their code works and a human judges the proof. Warp runs a cloud factory where agents never merge and a human touches every PR, with quality measured by a model reading the agent's own transcript. Vigil takes neither, because both let the thing that wrote the code produce the signal that it is good.
>
> **The gate is verification, not review.** A change lands when a process the worker cannot touch says it does: the verifier re-runs the contract's tests in a fresh checkout from the base's runner files, with hidden tests the worker never saw, and compares base, before and after. Review is a second signal with its own ladder. A worker's report, self-check and test claims are logged at zero weight. Nothing the worker can write, its tests included, decides whether code lands.
>
> **The branch is an implementation detail of the gate.** A `vigil/*` branch exists only between dispatch and verdict; the PR carries the verified commit and its audit record, kept because GitHub enforces checks at merge time and the owner's ruleset requires it. The tree the verifier passed is the tree that lands; a rebase re-verifies. A branch older than a day is a smell in the weekly report.
>
> **Autonomy is earned per change class.** Review and landing graduate by shape, measured on planted defects with a sequential test the owner can read: never by default, never for money, auth, consent, migrations or anything that changes the gate. The top rung, auto-land, is a predicate on the diff plus a verifier verdict plus a 48-hour window with one-regression demotion. Human merges are the floor Vigil starts from, not the ceiling.
>
> **The factory is code and runs where the code lives.** Routing, budgets, egress, review state and schedules are files in `configs/`, changed by PR. The daemon runs on the owner's machine; workers have no egress and no secrets; the only hosted thing is inference, metered through one gateway under hard dollar caps. Team access is a tailnet address and an MCP server, not a vendor's control plane. Where evidence is thin, Vigil measures on replays before it believes.

### (3) Five things Vigil should not adopt, ranked

1. **Amp's agent-produced proof as the gate** ("Give me irrefutable proof that this works now", [Amp](https://ampcode.com/time-capsules/6)): writer and prover are one system, the lever Anthropic measured as lenient and METR as hackable.
2. **Warp's transcript-read scorers as a quality signal** ([Warp](https://www.warp.dev/blog/agent-self-improving-software-factories)): a `tests_run` label graded from the worker's narration is the field Vigil forbids trusting.
3. **Amp's "unit tests might die" and reviewer-fixes-its-own-findings loop** ([Ball](https://thorstenball.com/blog/2026/09/19/what-i-believe-about-the-future-of-software-development/); [Amp](https://ampcode.com/news/liberating-code-review)): hidden tests in a fresh checkout are the one intervention measured to drive hacking near zero, and a reviewer that edits is a worker.
4. **Warp's cloud control plane with managed secrets granted to agents** ([Warp docs](https://docs.warp.dev/factories/factory-as-code/)): secrets entering the sandbox and model requests leaving the premises are the two properties Vigil's gateway and egress design exist to remove.
5. **Amp's rejection of token caps and permission boundaries as defaults** ([Amp](https://ampcode.com/docs/using-amp/how-we-work); [Amp](https://ampcode.com/news/neo)): Vigil's $5/day, $2/task and $100/month caps are hard stops and the container is the boundary; neither is friction to remove.

---

## Conclusion

Vigil is not "Warp with a stricter reviewer" or "Amp with a PR"; it is the only one of the three where the landing decision is made by a process the worker has no handle on, and that property, not the branch model or the hosting, is what the DORA, Faros and METR evidence rewards. Amp's trunk argument survives as a sizing discipline and a lifetime bound, not as the removal of a checkpoint; Warp's factory-as-code survives as two missing config files, not as a cloud dependency.

The builds are ordered: `review.yaml` and `automations.yaml` first, because V5's change classes and V4's flag-expiry scan read them; then the sizing rule on the existing spec gate; then chaining on the phase-2 scheduler; auto-land last, because its SPRT needs the planted-defect catalog and the 48-hour watcher that phases 2 and 6 own. Two numbers to watch once a class reaches rung 3: 17 consecutive clean trials is cheap for docs or lint and slow for tests-from-spec, and a reviewer at true recall 0.90 is promoted 57% of the time, so the planted-defect catalog decides what "recall" means more than the thresholds do. Nobody has published any of this; Vigil's weekly report can be the first place the numbers exist.
